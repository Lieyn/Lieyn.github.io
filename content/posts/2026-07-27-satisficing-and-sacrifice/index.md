As a chess instructor, I heard one question from students more than almost any other: "How do I make fewer mistakes?" My answers often varied: I would remind them of a mental checklist, suggest questions they could answer before making a move, or emphasize the importance of slowing down. There are countless reasons for mistakes and many ways to reduce them, but no method can prevent every error. For a while, the simple advice "be more careful" sufficed, a clear-cut answer that told students what they already knew. However, as I began to take on more students, different types of players kept coming back to the same question. Even grandmasters, who play the game for a living, do not play perfectly. A useful answer therefore has to help a developing player practice better habits while recognizing that mistakes will still happen. Why does knowing that we should be careful fail to prevent so many mistakes?

In general, and especially in chess, there is a common assumption about bad decisions: that they come from laziness, impulsivity, perhaps even a calculation mistake, or a failure to think carefully enough. Research suggests that some of the most predictable errors in human judgment come from the very shortcuts that usually help us decide efficiently. The world is too complex to calculate completely, and those shortcuts make decision-making possible even as they create the conditions for systematic mistakes.

To explore how we think, I consulted two papers in particular: Herbert Simon's "A Behavioral Model of Rational Choice" and Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman's "Judgment under Uncertainty." Together, the papers help explain why some errors follow predictable patterns: the shortcuts that make decisions manageable can also lead us to overlook relevant information.

## Simon: Rationality Under Limits

In 1955, Herbert Simon challenged the traditional economic model of decision-making, which imagined a rational agent who could identify every available option, calculate the consequences of each one, compare them, and choose the optimal result. However, Simon argued that this model was not realistic because human decisions happen under constraints. There are a multitude of limits when it comes to making our choices — limited time, limited information, limited attention, and limited cognitive capacity. For example, a chess player cannot calculate every possible move in any given situation. In one of the most ridiculous attempts possible, a chess amateur actually tried to surpass human limits by [attempting to beat world champion Magnus Carlsen](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MFNv-FJFGTg). There is always some uncertainty, no matter how small. We cannot be 100% certain of anything.

Rationality itself must be understood within limits. We try to make reasonable and rational choices, but we are just finite minds in a complex environment. Simon called this idea bounded rationality, a term he coined in *Models of Man*, because as humans, we are just problem-solvers who have limited information and limited mental capacities. The decision-making environment is reduced: we use shortcuts and cues, or universal rules that we deem more practical. In other words, we aim for a workable choice given the resources available.

Simon's primary alternative to optimization is "satisficing," a term he introduced in his 1956 paper, "Rational Choice and the Structure of the Environment." Combining the words "satisfy" and "suffice," satisficing describes a search for an acceptable option. Instead of searching for the best possible option, people often set a threshold and choose the first option that meets it. This implies that the ideal option isn't always available or selected; instead, we select one that is acceptable. We see real-world implications of this. Given the limited time in our lives, we must satisfice to be satisfied. That is just part of being human. From an economist's point of view, at some point, the marginal cost of the time we invest in searching for the best option can become higher than the value of finding something that is marginally better. We see this in our daily lives, whether it's spending hours comparing restaurants, pencils, or even optimal gym workouts. Hence, the advice to just try and start is effective. Satisficing allows people to act in the real world.

Simon also shows that decision-making is often a search process rather than a single comparison. People examine their possible options sequentially, using an aspiration level — a standard for what counts as good enough. Take, for example, students applying to college. College applicants often begin their search with a high standard, targeting schools that offer top academics, low costs, ideal locations, excellent career prospects, and a vibrant social scene. However, if no single university or college meets every criterion, students may adjust their expectations as they discover unexpected constraints and learn what is realistically achievable.

Decision-making becomes dynamic because people can revise what counts as good enough as they learn about the available options. Their ideal outcome may stay the same even while their acceptance threshold changes. Simon's model is important because it shows how simplifying a decision can make action possible under uncertainty.

## Kahneman and Tversky: When Shortcuts Become Biases

Nearly twenty years later, Kahneman and Tversky examined how mental shortcuts appear in real judgment. Because we cannot calculate everything, Kahneman and Tversky examined the mental techniques that people use in order to make effective choices. They found that the techniques people rely on most heavily are heuristics: shortcuts that help people make judgments quickly and, in many everyday situations, work well enough. Unsurprisingly, we tend to generalize when we use heuristics, which also makes them inherently faulty. Kahneman and Tversky focus on three major heuristics: representativeness, availability, and anchoring.

### Representativeness

Representativeness is the tendency to judge how likely something is by how closely it resembles a familiar example.

For example, we often associate people with occupations based on their characteristics. A person described as quiet, logical, and detail-oriented might be assumed to be a science enthusiast or a math-y person. What if, in fact, this person lived in a society where the ratio of scientists to farmers was 1:8? Is it still more likely for them to be a scientist than a farmer? Farmers greatly outnumber scientists in the relevant population, and that background information should also be taken into consideration. Although this does not discount informative details, we tend to give this description more weight than it deserves. Similarity does not imply probability; treating it as if it did is a pattern often labeled base-rate neglect in later literature, though Kahneman and Tversky's own heading for it is insensitivity to prior probability of outcomes. A base rate is the background frequency of something — the basic statistical information you should consider before looking at individual details — and people often use it correctly when no other information is given. But once they receive a description, even a weak or uninformative one, they may abandon the base rate and focus on the story instead. A story, in other words, can feel more informative than it really is.

Representativeness also helps explain why people misunderstand sample size. In their hospital example, Kahneman and Tversky described a large hospital that records about 45 births per day and a small hospital that records around 15 per day. People were asked which hospital would record more days on which more than 60% of the babies born were boys. Under the example's roughly 50–50 assumption, reaching at least 10 boys out of 15 is more likely than reaching at least 28 out of 45. Smaller samples have more extreme percentage swings. The smaller hospital is therefore expected to record more days above the threshold. At first, choosing the larger hospital may seem obvious and rational — a larger sample gives more opportunities for more boys to be born. However, larger samples actually reduce variability, while smaller samples show more extreme swings. More straightforwardly, getting at least 10 boys out of 15 births is more likely than getting at least 28 out of 45.

When a heuristic is applied, people tend to trust it more after a success and, conversely, less after a failure. Our brain wants smaller samples of success or failure to mean more than they actually do.

### Availability

Availability is the tendency to judge probability by how easily examples come to mind. If something is vivid, recent, emotional, or widely discussed, people tend to think it is more common or more likely than it actually is. For example, a dramatic plane crash can make flying feel especially dangerous because the crash is vivid and heavily reported. As I discussed in my [I-Search paper]({{< relref "2026-03-25-impulsivity-isearch" >}}), day trading can seem successful based on the supposed success of online traders.

Availability is not irrational in every case; often, things that come easily to mind really are common, and if you can quickly remember many examples of something, that can be useful evidence. However, memory is not a neutral and fair environment. It is shaped by attention, emotion, recency, media coverage, and personal experience. This is where the heuristic tends to fail — what is memorable can feel statistically meaningful even when it isn't.

If a chess player remembers an attacking win, that memory may make similar-looking attacks feel stronger than they are. Availability turns mental ease into perceived probability, but ease of retrieval is not the same as truth, frequency, or predictive value.

### Anchoring

Anchoring is defined as the tendency to rely too heavily on an initial value. When people estimate an unknown quantity, they often begin with a number that is already available and adjust from it. More often than not, the adjustment is insufficient, which suggests that our initial judgment tends to shape our final one even when it should not.

Anchoring appears in many settings: in negotiation, the first offer influences the final price; in real estate, the listing price shapes what buyers consider reasonable; in forecasting, an initial estimate can pull later estimates toward it. Even arbitrary numbers can affect judgment once they become the starting point. In Tversky and Kahneman's best-known demonstration, participants watched a wheel of fortune land on a number between 0 and 100 and were then asked to estimate the percentage of African countries in the United Nations. Those who saw 10 gave a median estimate of 25%, while those who saw 65 gave a median estimate of 45%, even though the wheel obviously had nothing to do with the answer. This matters because it shows that human judgment is not formed from scratch but is often built around whatever reference point appears first.

From an economic and psychological lens, marketers and people bargaining tend to target this heuristic. When a retailer lists a jacket as "\\\$500 marked down to \\\$250," the \\\$500 acts as an economic anchor. Even if the jacket is only worth \\\$200 in raw utility, the initial high price skews your internal reference price. If an employer asks, "Were you expecting around \\\$60,000?", that low number anchors the entire negotiation. The candidate then adjusts upward, but psychological inertia keeps the final counter-offer near \\\$65,000 rather than the true market value of \\\$80,000.

Anchoring can be viewed alongside Simon's account of limited decision-making: a starting point helps organize an uncertain problem but may also influence the answer too strongly. Bounded agents need somewhere to begin. Using a starting point can make thinking manageable; relying on it too heavily can bias the result.

## Relationship between the Two

The relationship between these two papers is not simply that Simon says "people are limited" and Kahneman and Tversky say "people make mistakes." The two accounts can be read together, although they examine different parts of decision-making: Simon emphasizes search and stopping rules, while Kahneman and Tversky examine shortcuts in judgment. Choosing when to stop searching, recognizing a familiar pattern, and estimating how likely something is are related but distinct processes.

A shortcut can be useful in some settings and systematically misleading in others. Gigerenzer and Goldstein (1996) later pushed this point further, showing that simple "fast and frugal" heuristics can perform as well as far more complex strategies when they fit the structure of the environment. The question is whether the cue a shortcut uses — resemblance, ease of recall, or a starting value — provides reliable evidence for this particular decision. A heuristic is a strategy for judging or choosing; a bias is a systematic tendency for judgment to depart from an appropriate standard. Representativeness is useful when surface resemblance tracks deeper similarity, but fails when two things look alike while differing in the details that matter. Availability is useful when memory reflects frequency, but fails when vividness or recency distorts what comes to mind. A starting estimate can organize a problem, but anchoring becomes a concern when that estimate receives too much weight.

However, we cannot just eliminate shortcuts. The better goal is to understand the conditions under which a shortcut should be trusted. If an error follows a predictable pattern, that gives us a starting point for designing a check. Whether a particular check improves decisions still needs to be tested: recognizing a bias does not automatically make it easy to avoid.

## What This Looks Like in Chess

Chess makes this tension precise because chess is too complex for complete calculation, but structured enough for mistakes to be studied. A chess sacrifice means deliberately giving up a piece or other material to gain something in return, such as a strong attack. The player must decide whether that expected benefit is worth the immediate loss. When deciding whether to sacrifice a piece, a player cannot calculate every possible continuation, so the player relies on pattern recognition, positional intuition, and a rough sense of whether the resulting position looks dangerous for the opponent. That is an example of bounded rationality: the player simplifies because full calculation is impossible. If the player stops searching after finding a move that meets a threshold for being playable or promising, that stopping rule is an example of satisficing.

But this is exactly where Kahneman and Tversky become relevant. A position may look like a successful sacrifice from a previous game, but resemblance is not the same as equivalence. An attack may look like a famous winning combination, but fail because the opponent has an escape route or an extra defender. The overall shape is familiar; the decisive detail is different.

For readers familiar with chess notation, a bishop sacrifice on h7 may resemble the classic [Greek Gift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_gift_sacrifice) pattern, and yet the details matter more than the resemblance does. Can the knight reach g5? Can the queen reach h5? Is f6 free of a defending knight? Is the opposing king without an escape square? Is the opponent unable to decline the sacrifice safely? Is there actually enough follow-up after the first check? If those conditions are missing, the sacrifice may only look like a winning pattern without actually being one. Trusting the familiar appearance without checking those details is what representativeness bias looks like on the chessboard. The move alone does not reveal whether a player did this, but either way, the better question is not just "have I seen this before?" but "is this position similar in the ways that actually matter?"

One possible advantage of stronger players is that they check whether the familiar pattern's key conditions are present. The pattern is still useful, but the player can ask a second question: what exactly is the compensation — what is gained in return for the sacrificed piece — and is this position truly similar to the ones where the sacrifice succeeded? That second question offers a possible correction. It keeps the heuristic while making its conditions explicit.

An unpublished analysis conducted with my mentor, submitted to the *International Journal of High School Research* (IJHSR), examines a related question: do higher-rated players select sounder sacrifices? In this paper, I model a chess sacrifice as a risky decision under uncertainty: a player receives a noisy private signal about a position's quality and decides whether to commit material on the basis of that signal. Although the board is visible to both players, the uncertainty lies in the player's incomplete assessment of what may follow.

The model treats a player's assessment as the position's underlying chance of success plus an error. More precise assessment means smaller errors on average. These private assessments are theoretical quantities; the game records do not directly reveal them. Formally, I represent a player's read of a position as

$$
s=\theta+\varepsilon,\qquad \varepsilon\sim N(0,\sigma^2),
$$

where $\theta$ is the position's underlying probability of success in the model, $s$ is the player's noisy signal, and $\varepsilon$ is the assessment error. The model defines signal precision as $1/\sigma^2$. A high-precision signal produces an intuition that tracks the position more reliably; a low-precision signal produces a noisier assessment. Because the normal error is unbounded, $s$ can fall outside 0–1, so it is best read as a raw signal rather than as a probability estimate itself.

The game analysis examines the relationship between rating and sacrifice soundness, rather than directly measuring signal precision. Across roughly 75,000 classical games, which yield 35,830 sacrifices that pass my filters, I found a positive linear association across the sampled rating range. After controlling for the engine's evaluation of the position before the sacrifice, sacrifice soundness improves by about 1.5 centipawns for every 100 rating points of the player making the sacrifice ($p\approx10^{-45}$, $R^2=0.705$, OLS with standard errors clustered by game).

In plain language, higher-rated players tended to choose sacrifices that the chess engine judged more favorably. A [centipawn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centipawn) is one-hundredth of a pawn in an engine's evaluation scale, not a percentage point of winning probability, so the estimated difference is 0.015 pawns per 100 rating points. That is a modest effect, and it describes an average association, not a guarantee about an individual move.

Two of those numbers are easy to over-read. With more than 35,000 sacrifices, even a small slope can produce a tiny p-value: it is strong evidence that the relationship is not zero, not evidence that it is large, and it does not identify a psychological mechanism. Similarly, most of the $R^2$ comes from the position's evaluation before the sacrifice, not from rating, so it does not mean that rating explains 70% of sacrifice quality. Clustering by game accounts for the fact that sacrifices from the same game are not independent observations.

This result does not establish an elite-only threshold or rule out nonlinear patterns within the sample. Nor does it explain why stronger players select sounder sacrifices. One possibility is the "second question" account developed above: stronger players check the conditions of a familiar pattern more carefully before committing. Another comes from Chase and Simon's 1973 work on chunking ("Perception in Chess," summarized for a general audience in "Skill in Chess"). Their reconstruction task examined how players recalled briefly shown positions. Patterns in the order and timing of recalled pieces supported the idea that experienced players remember meaningful clusters, or "chunks," rather than treating every piece as an isolated item.

If experience helps players recognize meaningful groups of pieces, that points to a different explanation for stronger play: experience may create a denser and more accurate library of patterns, so resemblance is more likely to track the features that matter. A more useful pattern library could support better sacrifices without requiring more explicit checking on every move.

Both explanations could produce the same positive association between rating and sacrifice soundness, and my regression cannot distinguish them. The narrow conclusion is that stronger players in my sample selected sounder sacrifices. The analysis does not directly measure players' private evaluations, confidence, reliance on representativeness, or calibration. A further possibility is that stronger players require more evidence before sacrificing at all. That would change which sacrifices appear in the sample without, by itself, showing either more accurate evaluation or a different appetite for risk. The reported association does not separate evaluation precision from risk preference.

This distinction matters because signal precision and aspiration level describe different parts of a decision. Precision describes how closely a player's assessment tracks a position's underlying soundness: how much noise exists in the read. An aspiration level describes the threshold at which the player stops searching and commits to a move. Better information can support a better threshold, but it does not determine that threshold. A player can evaluate a position accurately yet demand too much or too little compensation before sacrificing; a conservative threshold cannot remove noise from an inaccurate evaluation.

For example, two players might both estimate that an attack has a 60% chance of working. One accepts that chance; the other wants at least 80% before giving up a piece. They share the same assessment but use different decision thresholds. (These numbers are illustrative, not results from the study.)

Decision quality over repeated positions depends on both the assessment and the rule used to act on it. In my chess model, precision is an information parameter, while aspiration level is a decision threshold. They can vary separately and should not be treated as interchangeable.

## The Broader Point

This brings me back to the question my students asked most often: "How do I make fewer mistakes?" There is no checklist that can eliminate mistakes, and even grandmasters do not play perfectly. The goal is not to calculate everything or distrust every instinct. A practical starting point is to notice when an instinct is carrying the decision and to pause long enough to test it. Before sacrificing, a player can ask: Which detail differs from the pattern I recognize? What do I gain in return for the piece? What is my opponent's strongest response?

Asking these questions is a practical habit worth testing because it directs attention toward the details on which the decision depends. My analysis shows that stronger players in the sample selected sounder sacrifices, but not necessarily why. A next step would be to test whether teaching this checking habit actually improves sacrifice quality.

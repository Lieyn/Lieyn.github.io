As a chess instructor, one of the most common questions I would get from students is: "How do I make less mistakes?" My answers often varied, I would remind them of a mental checklist to make, questions they could answer before making a move, reminding them the importance of slowing down, and many more. There are countless reasons and countless ways in order one could theoretically prevent a mistake, but there are just so many factors that mistakes are just inevitable. For a while, the simple "be more careful" sufficed, a clear cut answer that told students what they already knew. However, as I began to take on more students, different archetypes of players kept coming back to the same question. In fact, is there even an answer? Grandmasters, players who play the game for a living, with all their knowledge, do they even have answers? Grandmasters don't play perfectly; they just blunder less severely and respond to their errors more resiliently than amateurs. When the question is reframed this way, a developing player can practice more efficient habits. But why is there no answer, what goes on behind the scenes?

In general and especially in chess, there is a common assumption about bad decisions: that they come from laziness, impulsivity, perhaps even a calculation mistake, or a failure to think carefully enough. Research suggests something else, in fact, some of the most predictable errors in human judgment do not come from people refusing to think — they come from people thinking efficiently. Our brain practices efficiency, we rely on mental shortcuts because the world is too complex to calculate completely, and those shortcuts make decision-making possible even as they create the conditions for systematic mistakes.

Two papers, written almost twenty years apart, explain this tension. Herbert Simon's "A Behavioral Model of Rational Choice" explains why shortcuts are necessary. Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky's "Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases" explains why those same shortcuts can fail. Together, the papers suggest that human error is not random but structured: mistakes occur because of the way we think, the tools that we use to make choices rather than because we don't think enough.

## Simon: Rationality Under Limits

In 1955, Herbert Simon challenged the traditional economic model of decision-making, which imagined a rational agent who could identify every available option, calculate the consequences of each one, compare them, and choose the optimal result. However, Simon argued that this model was simply not realistic. In humans, real decisions happen under constraints — limited time, limited information, limited attention, and limited cognitive capacity. For example, a chess player cannot calculate every possible line to checkmate. An investor cannot model every variable that might affect a stock. A doctor cannot run every possible test before making a choice, and even a student choosing a college can't predict how their future will look like, even if the institution is prestigious.

Simon's point was not that people are irrational, but that rationality itself must be understood within limits: humans try to make reasonable choices, but they do so as finite minds in complex environments. This is the idea now known as bounded rationality, a term which Simon did not actually coin until *Models of Man* two years later, but the claim itself is already fully worked out here: people are not perfect optimizers but problem-solvers working with incomplete information and limited mental resources. Because of this, they simplify the decision environment, reducing the number of options, focusing on the most relevant cues, and using rules that are practical rather than perfect. In essence, practical choices over perfect.

Simon's primary alternative to optimization is "satisficing," a term he introduced in his 1956 paper, "Rational Choice and the Structure of the Environment." Combining the words "satisfy" and "suffice," the core mechanism of this concept was already fully developed in his earlier work. Instead of searching for the best possible option, people often set a threshold and choose the first option that meets it, so the search stops not when the ideal answer has been found, but when an acceptable one has. This is not a weakness so much as the only workable strategy in many situations: if a person searched endlessly for the best possible job, house, investment, or chess move, they might never act at all, because the cost of searching can become higher than the value of finding something marginally better. Satisficing allows people to act in the real world.

Simon also shows that decision-making is often a search process rather than a single comparison. People examine their possible options sequentially, using a measure of standard determined by their aspirations — for what is determined as being good enough. Take for example, students applying to college. College applicants often begin their search with a high standard, targeting schools that offer top academics, low costs, ideal locations, excellent career prospects, and a vibrant social scene. However, if no single institution meets every criterion, students typically adjust their expectations as they discover unexpected constraints and learn what is realistically achievable.

Decision-making becomes dynamic rather than fixed because preferences are not fixed, they are constantly being revised and adjusted to ideal parameters set by the individual. As more constraints are revealed, we change our standards accordingly. Simon's model is important because shortcuts are necessary tools — the infrastructure of decision-making under uncertainty rather than a weakness.

## Kahneman and Tversky: When Shortcuts Become Biases

Twenty years later Kahneman and Tversky examined how those shortcuts actually appear in real judgment. If Simon explains why humans cannot calculate everything, Kahneman and Tversky ask a question: when people simplify uncertain situations, what specific mental shortcuts do they use and where do those shortcuts go wrong? Kahneman and Tversky answer that people rely heavily on heuristics — shortcuts that help people make judgments quickly and in many everyday situations work well enough. The problem is that those heuristics can also produce predictable errors. Kahneman and Tversky focus on major heuristics: representativeness, availability, and anchoring.

### Representativeness

Representativeness is defined as the tendency to associate probability by similarity. For example, instead we may make choices of what the outcome looks like rather than the probable or improbable choices. Sometimes this can be useful because we can compare outcomes to familiar situations, however it can become dangerous when people ignore important statistical data and information.

In terms of examples, we tend to associate groups of people to occupations or more based on characteristics. Take, for example, a person who is described as quiet, precise, and detail-oriented, you might describe this person as a librarian, or a computer scientist. Even if the majority of the population are farmers, it still might seem more likely that this person is a different occupation, even though the statistical probability favors a farmer. The mistake comes from treating similarity as if it were probability — a pattern often labeled base-rate neglect in later literature, though Kahneman and Tversky's own heading for it is insensitivity to prior probability of outcomes. A base rate is the background frequency of something, the basic statistical information you should consider before looking at individual details, and people often use it correctly when no other information is given. But once they receive a description, even a weak or uninformative one, they tend to abandon the base rate and focus on the story instead. A story, in other words, can feel more informative than it really is.

Representativeness also explains why people misunderstand sample size, which Kahneman and Tversky's hospital example shows clearly. A large hospital records about 45 births per day, a small hospital about 15, and people are asked which hospital will record more days when over 60% of the babies born are boys. Many people answer that both hospitals should have about the same number of such days, but the correct answer is the smaller hospital: small samples vary more, so it is much easier for 10 out of 15 babies to be boys than for 28 out of 45, and larger samples tend to stay closer to the population average. The mistake happens because people focus on whether "more than 60% boys" seems representative of randomness in general, and fail to account for how sample size changes what counts as a plausible fluctuation.

This has real-life consequences, because people overreact to small amounts of evidence all the time. A student gets one bad test grade and thinks they are bad at the subject. A company sees one successful marketing campaign and assumes the same strategy will always work. A chess player wins one game with a risky sacrifice and begins to overtrust the pattern. The mind wants small samples to mean more than they do.

### Availability

Availability is the tendency to judge probability by how easily examples come to mind. If something is vivid, recent, emotional, or widely discussed, people tend to think it is more common or more likely than it actually is. For example, a dramatic plane crash can feel more dangerous than ordinary driving simply because it is vivid and heavily reported.

Availability is not irrational in every case; often, things that come easily to mind really are common, and if you can quickly remember many examples of something, that can be useful evidence. But memory is not a neutral database. It is shaped by attention, emotion, recency, media coverage, and personal experience, and that is where the heuristic fails: what is memorable can feel statistically meaningful even when it isn't.

If a chess player remembers an attacking win, that memory may make similar-looking attacks feel stronger than they are. Availability turns mental ease into perceived probability, but ease of retrieval is not the same as truth, frequency, or predictive value.

### Anchoring

Anchoring is defined as the tendency to rely too heavily on an initial value. When people estimate an unknown quantity, they often begin with a number that is already available and adjust from it — and the problem is that the adjustment is usually insufficient, so the starting point continues to shape the final judgment even when it should not.

Anchoring appears in many settings: in negotiation, the first offer influences the final price; in real estate, the listing price shapes what buyers consider reasonable; in forecasting, an initial estimate can pull later estimates toward it. Even arbitrary numbers can affect judgment once they become the starting point, which matters because it shows that human judgment is not formed from scratch but is often built around whatever reference point appears first.

This connects back to Simon. If people had unlimited computational ability, arbitrary starting points would not matter much, because they would calculate the correct answer independently. But bounded agents need somewhere to begin, and anchors help structure an uncertain problem even as they distort the final judgment. The same tool that makes thinking manageable can also bias the result.

## Relationship between the Two

The relationship between these two papers is not simply that Simon says "people are limited" and Kahneman and Tversky say "people make mistakes." The researchers work together. Simon provides the foundation: human beings cannot optimize perfectly because real decision-making exceeds our cognitive capacity, so we simplify. Kahneman and Tversky provide the psychology: when people simplify, they often rely on resemblance, memory, and anchors, and while these shortcuts are useful, they create predictable errors.

Simon might look at Kahneman and Tversky's findings and say: of course people use shortcuts, since a finite mind in a complex world has no other choice. But he would probably also recognize that not every shortcut is equally reliable due to the environment, naturally having many factors. A shortcut is not bad itself, it only becomes a bias when it is applied in a situation where it does not directly apply to the situation — in other words, incorrect calibration. Representativeness is useful when surface resemblance tracks deeper similarity, but fails when two things look alike while differing in the details that matter. Availability is useful when memory reflects frequency, but fails when vividness or recency distorts what comes to mind. Anchoring is useful because people need starting points, but fails when the starting point is arbitrary or overweighted.

However, we cannot just eliminate shortcuts. The better goal is to understand the conditions under which a shortcut should be trusted. If the errors are systematic, they are learnable, which is the hopeful part of Kahneman and Tversky's paper: human judgment has weaknesses, but those weaknesses are patterned, and if we know the patterns, we can build checks around them.

## What This Looks Like in Chess

Chess makes this tension precise because chess is too complex for complete calculation, but structured enough for mistakes to be studied. When deciding whether to sacrifice a piece, a player cannot calculate every possible continuation, so the player relies on pattern recognition, positional intuition, and a rough sense of whether the resulting position looks dangerous for the opponent. That is Simon's bounded rationality in action: the player is not being lazy, but simplifying because full calculation is impossible, satisficing by asking whether a move appears to meet the threshold for being playable, dangerous, or promising.

But this is exactly where Kahneman and Tversky become relevant. A position may look like a successful sacrifice from a previous game, but resemblance is not the same as equivalence. A bishop sacrifice on h7 may resemble the classic Greek Gift pattern, and yet the details matter more than the resemblance does. Can the knight reach g5? Can the queen reach h5? Is the defender's knight on f6? Does the opposing king have an escape square? Can the opponent decline the sacrifice? Is there actually enough follow-up after the first check? If those details are missing, the sacrifice may only look like a winning pattern without actually being one. That is representativeness bias on the chessboard: the position feels familiar, so the player trusts the pattern too quickly, when the better question is not "have I seen this before?" but "is this position similar in the ways that actually matter?"

A weak player may see a familiar attacking shape and sacrifice immediately. A stronger player does not abandon the pattern — the pattern is still useful — but checks the conditions under which it works, asking a second question: what exactly is the compensation, and is this position truly similar to the ones where the sacrifice succeeded? That second question is the correction. It does not eliminate the heuristic; it disciplines it.

I tested this hypothesis in an unpublished analysis conducted with my mentor, which has been submitted to IJHSR and is not yet public. In this paper, I model a chess sacrifice as a risky decision under uncertainty: a player receives a noisy private signal about a position's quality and decides whether to commit material on the basis of that signal.

Formally, I represent a player's read of a position as

$$
s=p+\varepsilon,\qquad \varepsilon\sim N(0,\sigma^2),
$$

where $p$ is the position's true probability of success. The model defines signal precision as $1/\sigma^2$. A high-precision signal produces an intuition that tracks the position more reliably; a low-precision signal produces a noisier assessment.

If the disciplined-heuristic account is correct, sacrifice quality should increase with rating rather than appearing only among a small group of elite players. Across roughly 75,000 classical games, I find a positive linear association across the sampled rating range: sacrifice soundness improves by about 1.5 centipawns for every 100 rating points of the player making the sacrifice ($p\approx10^{-45}$, $R^2=0.705$, cluster-robust OLS).

This result does not establish an elite-only threshold or rule out nonlinear patterns within the sample. Nor does it explain why stronger players select sounder sacrifices. One possibility is the "second question" account developed above: stronger players check the conditions of a familiar pattern more carefully before committing. Another comes from Chase and Simon's 1973 chunking experiment, "Perception in Chess." In that study, a chess master and a Class A player reconstructed briefly shown positions. The master was not simply better at memorizing individual squares; the pauses between placed pieces indicated that he recalled larger chunks — recurring clusters of pieces — and rebuilt positions chunk by chunk rather than piece by piece.

The broader skill gradient often associated with this result — grandmasters and masters recalling positions nearly perfectly, experts performing less well, and novices recalling only a few pieces — comes from de Groot's earlier work, which Simon and Chase extended and replicated. This suggests a different explanation for stronger play: experience may create a denser and more accurate library of patterns, so resemblance is more likely to track the features that matter.

Both explanations could produce the same positive association between rating and sacrifice soundness, and my regression cannot distinguish them. The narrow conclusion is that stronger players in my sample selected sounder sacrifices. The analysis does not directly measure players' private evaluations, confidence, reliance on representativeness, or calibration. A further possibility is that stronger players use a stricter threshold for sacrificing at all, which isn't a testament towards evaluation accuracy, but towards risk tolerance. Calibration remains a possible interpretation, not a tested conclusion.

This distinction matters because signal precision and aspiration level describe different parts of a decision. Precision describes how closely a player's assessment tracks a position's underlying soundness: how much noise exists in the read. An aspiration level describes the threshold at which the player stops searching and commits to a move. Better information can support a better threshold, but it does not determine that threshold. A player can evaluate a position accurately yet demand too much or too little compensation before sacrificing; a conservative threshold cannot remove noise from an inaccurate evaluation.

Whether these features produce good outcomes over repeated positions, calibration depends on both. In my chess model, precision is an information parameter, while aspiration level is a decision threshold. They can vary separately and should not be treated as interchangeable.

## The Broader Point: Structured Intuition

This brings me back to the question my students asked most often: "How do I make fewer mistakes?" There is no checklist that can eliminate mistakes, and even grandmasters do not play perfectly. The goal is not to calculate everything or distrust every instinct. It is to notice when an instinct is carrying the decision and to pause long enough to test it. Before sacrificing, a player can ask: What makes this position different from the pattern I recognize? What is my actual compensation? What move has my opponent been waiting for?

That is what better judgment looks like in chess and beyond it. Learning to ask that second question is how players make fewer mistakes over time.

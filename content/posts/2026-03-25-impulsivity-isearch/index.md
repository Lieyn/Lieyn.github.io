## Introduction

Like most people, I am guilty of mindlessly scrolling on social media, sometimes not even processing what reels or content I see. Every dull moment — waiting in the lunch line, sitting in class waiting for the day to end, and even in the bathroom — I automatically reach for my phone. Ironically enough, it was during one of those sessions that I observed the development of certain trends. After encountering numerous online casino advertisements, I noticed that more and more influencers seemed to be promoting platforms such as Rainbet or Stake. I also noticed influencers, particularly day trading influencers, advertising courses promising financial freedom with only a few dedicated hours. Essentially, these promotions encourage the misleading notion that you can watch a few videos, put in some hours, and magically become rich.

Nowadays, it seems like everyone is gambling, whether it's in financial markets they know nothing about or prediction markets, offering bets on the weather, and even their health — through the promotion of peptides and research chemicals. Seemingly, this suggests that our digital habits can actually influence financial behavior. Although it may appear that the fault lies within the ethics of these companies, research suggests that impulsivity and the preference for immediate rewards also deserve attention. In this essay, digital overstimulation refers to frequent exposure to rapidly changing, attention-grabbing content, rather than a measured neurological condition. Research links heavier smartphone use with a stronger preference for immediate rewards, while other studies connect impulsivity with gambling problems. These findings do not prove that scrolling causes harmful financial decisions, but they raise concerns about digital environments that combine constant stimulation with easy access to financial risks. Finance and technology companies can profit from these environments, making their design and marketing practices important to examine, especially where adolescents are concerned.

## Understanding the Marketing Behind Side Hustles

To understand the implications of this problem, we first need to understand how these markets are designed and how creators promote these schemes. In a quote from Daniel Chemtob's informational article, "Get Rich Quick Schemes For The TikTok Generation," Nate Hoskin reveals that the main issue "with side hustle culture on TikTok is the fact that it is often easier to teach someone how to side hustle than it is to actually do the side hustle" (Chemtob). In other words, Hoskin indicates that the real profit driven by the side hustle industry is in selling the idea of the hustle rather than the hustle itself. These promotions appeal to the desire for quick rewards. Adults and adolescents may purchase misleading courses or guides because they promise a shortcut to success. The marketing encourages viewers to focus on the end result before assessing the legitimacy of the process they are investing in. For example, Jillian Randal, who failed at the side hustle she promoted, has made up to $17,000 a month by simply selling courses on how to be successful in that side hustle. Her success illustrates how profitable selling the promise of easy money can be.

Earnings from selling courses can also create the appearance that the underlying side hustle works. Viewers may mistake a promoter's wealth for evidence that the method being taught is successful. Repeated exposure to get-rich-quick content can keep these promises visible. Chemtob's article mentioned a victim of these schemes, Ryan, who simplified the concept: "People have figured out, these get attention and get people to watch," he says. "Easy money is just appealing" (Chemtob). His observation highlights the incentive for creators to keep promoting the same promise: easy money attracts attention.

Other means of appealing to viewers can include a fake online persona, which incorporates a lavish lifestyle that seemingly provides the promoter credibility to sell their courses. Images of luxury cars or first-class travel can make a promoted activity appear more profitable than it is. Chemtob's article reveals how these schemes appeal to human curiosity and the desire for financial success. This helps explain what viewers are being offered; research on decision-making helps explain why immediate rewards can be so appealing.

## Our Brain and the Appeal of Immediate Rewards

Even though it may seem that only irrational individuals fall for such schemes, research on decision-making offers reasons to look beyond personal character flaws. In his TED Talk, "Snapgrat: How Instant Gratification Is Harming Society and What to Do About It," John Davidson argues that modern technology has caused the erosion of society's tolerance for delayed gratification. His argument raises a useful question about how constant access to stimulation might affect our willingness to wait. Research on how people value rewards provides a starting point for examining that question.

Furthermore, in his article "Of Two Minds," Paul Massari explores the mechanics behind human decision-making, presenting Professor David Laibson's account of the roles of the mesolimbic dopamine reward system (MDR) and the prefrontal cortex (PFC). In this account, the MDR is associated with reward processing, while the PFC contributes to planning and cognitive control. This is a simplified model of decision-making, rather than a complete account of how the brain makes choices. Discussing Laibson's behavioral economics research, Massari quotes him on the discovery that "there's a high rate of discounting in the short run, then virtually no discounting as people look further into the future" (Massari). This is directly connected to the idea of hyperbolic discounting. Unlike exponential discounting — where individuals value future rewards less by a constant rate over time, producing decisions that stay consistent no matter when they're made — hyperbolic discounting means the discount rate itself falls as the delay grows. For example, someone might choose $110 in 372 days over $100 in 365 days, but choose $100 today over $110 in 7 days — same 7-day gap, same $10 premium, opposite choice. This reversal illustrates the time inconsistency that hyperbolic discounting can explain. Under a fixed exponential discounting model, shifting both rewards equally in time would not reverse their ranking. Massari's account of Laibson's research offers a possible explanation for why immediate rewards sometimes receive disproportionate weight. Explaining this appeal, Laibson states "The MDR discounts rapidly. It puts a lot of weight on the present, but little on the future" (Massari). This model helps explain the appeal of rewards available now compared with those that require waiting. Get-rich-quick schemes promise to shorten that wait, which may make them attractive even when their claims deserve scrutiny. However, preferring an earlier reward is not the same as accepting a risky bet. To connect this model to financial harm, we also need evidence about impulsivity and gambling behavior.

## Empirical Evidence: Observations and Analysis

Empirical studies provide evidence of an association between smartphone use and impulsive choice. In their PLOS One study "Likes and Impulsivity," Schulz van Endert and Mohr observed whether digital screen time correlates with discounting, the tendency to prefer immediate rewards over long-term larger rewards. In a sample of 101 participants, heavier smartphone use was associated with a greater preference for smaller immediate rewards. Through actual usage data, researchers were able to perceive how "smartphone screen time was correlated with choosing smaller immediate over larger delayed rewards" (Schulz van Endert and Mohr). They also found that the "usage of social media and gaming apps predicted delay discounting" (Schulz van Endert and Mohr). These findings connect measured smartphone use with immediate-reward preference. They do not establish that screen time caused this preference or that participants became more attracted to day trading or online gambling. People who already prefer immediate rewards might also spend more time on their phones. Both behaviors could also reflect other influences. This limits the causal conclusion we can draw, but it does not make the association irrelevant.

The authors also discuss earlier research linking delay discounting with behaviors such as problematic drinking and gambling. Intertemporal choice refers to decisions whose costs or benefits occur at different times. These choices depend in part on how an individual evaluates rewards. This makes delay discounting relevant to financial behavior, while leaving open the question of how smartphone use fits into that relationship.

In addition, a longitudinal study published in *Frontiers in Psychology*, "The Relationship Between Impulsivity and Problem Gambling in Adolescence," tracked 874 high school students across different measures of impulsivity. The researchers found that one self-reported measure of impulsivity predicted later gambling problems, and their overall findings suggested that impulsivity and problem gambling may reinforce one another (Secades-Villa et al.). This supports concern about financial risks among impulsive adolescents. However, the study did not identify digital overstimulation as the source of their impulsivity. Together, the two studies suggest a possible connection worth investigating, rather than a proven pipeline from scrolling to gambling. That distinction still leaves an important question: how do industries market risky activities to people who may be vulnerable to their appeal?

## Industry Exploitation

The design of online gambling platforms raises concerns about how the gambling, financial, and technology industries profit from immediate-reward behavior. In her *Harvard Gazette* article "Gambling Problems Are Mushrooming," Clea Simon documents that Harvard-affiliated public health experts argue that rapid technological advancement has created unprecedented problems with youth gambling and access. One expert quoted in Simon's article explains, "Tech makes sure the experience is seamless; the gambling industry makes it tantalizing; and the financial industry makes it possible to put your money in with just a click or a tap" (Simon). This account shows how three industries can work together to make gambling appealing and easy to access. Removing steps between an urge and a bet may reduce opportunities to pause, though it does not eliminate a user's ability to make a considered choice.

Simon's article also identifies emerging adults and adolescents as groups at particular risk and discusses concerns that workarounds such as sweepstakes framing can undermine age restrictions (Simon). These concerns support closer scrutiny of youth access and marketing practices. A speaker in NPR journalist Patrick Madden's report captures the concern: "Take the most addictive behaviors in mankind and then combine it with one of the most addicting devices of all time. And, you know, it's obviously going to be a disaster" (Madden). This warning highlights the risks of combining gambling with a device that is always within reach.

Treatment-referral data also illustrate the scale of concern. Madden reports that "the total number of residents in their 20s and 30s who contacted the hotline and were referred to gambling treatment has more than doubled since the state legalized sports betting in 2023" (Madden). The increase points to greater demand for gambling treatment in that setting, but it does not identify digital overstimulation as the cause. Greater access to betting or greater awareness of treatment could also contribute. Even with that limitation, the report raises a practical question: how should society respond when gambling becomes easier to access and more widely promoted? The ease of moving from a promotion to a bet makes platform design an important part of that discussion.

## Conclusion

Social media is part of everyday life for many young adults and adolescents. Backed by studies of smartphone use and gambling behavior, there is reason to examine how digital environments interact with impulsive financial choices. The evidence reviewed here links heavier smartphone use with immediate-reward preference and connects impulsivity with gambling problems. The full causal pathway remains uncertain, but the overlap between these behaviors deserves attention. Beyond recognizable behavior, the concern becomes alarming because of how easily entertainment, promises of quick wealth, and opportunities to gamble can appear in the same digital space. In the short term, digital literacy and awareness could help people assess these promotions and recognize the risks involved. However, in the long term individual awareness should be accompanied by scrutiny of platform design and marketing practices. Financial education, clearer disclosure of promotional incentives, and stronger protections against youth access are possible responses that deserve examination.
